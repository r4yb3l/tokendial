If you have been coding with an agent for a while you already know the shape of it. The code comes out
fine, the logic comes out fine, and the interface comes out **the same**. White background, cards with
rounded corners, a purple-to-blue gradient in the hero, Inter everywhere, and that blue-grey you can spot
from a mile away. It is not that the model cannot design. It is that "make it look good" is zero
information, and what comes back is the statistical average of every landing page it has ever seen.

The obvious answer is to describe the style yourself. The problem is that describing a whole design system
— palette, type scale, radii, shadows, how every component behaves, transitions — is hours of work, and you
redo it in every project. designprompts.dev has already done it thirty times.

## What it actually is

Someone took one fictional landing page — a company called Acme Inc., with a hero, statistics, features,
blog, pricing and an FAQ — and rendered it thirty times, once per visual system: Monochrome, Bauhaus,
Terminal, Cyberpunk, Neo-brutalism, Academia, Vaporwave, Art Deco and on to thirty. You move through the
list with the arrow keys and see each version whole and alive inside a fake browser frame. They are not
screenshots: they are real pages, and you can open one in its own tab and scroll to the footer.

When one of them fits, you press **Get Prompt**. What comes out is neither an image nor a Tailwind theme:
it is a text document in two parts. The first defines who the model should be and orders it to read your
stack before writing anything. The second is the style specification in Markdown, with the exact hexes, the
full type scale, border weights, textures and their opacity, how every button and input behaves, and what
happens on focus and on hover.

## The part worth stealing

What makes those documents work is not the level of detail. It is that each one carries an explicit list of
what the style **is not**. Monochrome states that it has no gradients, no shadows, no rounded corners, and
— word for word — that it must not look like "Minimalist Modern with the colours removed".

A generative model obeys a concrete prohibition far better than an adjective. That is the technique, and it
transfers to every design prompt you write from here on, whether or not you ever use one of these thirty.

## Using it without burning your context

Copy and paste works for a demo and fails for a project. This is the flow that pays off.

1. **Choose by looking at the demo, not at the name.** "Professional", "Enterprise", "SaaS" and "Modern
   Dark" sound alike and are four different things. Open the full page and scroll down to pricing and the
   FAQ: that is where a visual system falls apart, not in the hero.
2. **Save it as a file in the repository, not as a chat message.** Put it in `docs/design-system.md` and
   reference it from your `CLAUDE.md` or your editor's rules. The style then survives a session restart,
   you are not resending it every turn, and you can edit it the day you decide that a zero radius does not
   apply to your modals.
3. **Answer the interview before it asks.** The prompt is written to slot into code that already exists, so
   its first move is to ask you about your stack, scope and constraints. Paste it expecting diffs and you
   will get questions instead. Get ahead of it in the same message and you save two full turns.
4. **Apply it to one screen and use that screen as the reference.** These are ambitious documents: they ask
   for layered textures, enormous type, non-negotiable decisions. Applying one to a whole app at once
   produces a redesign nobody can review. Leave one screen impeccable and let it be the example for the
   rest; from there you are working against your own token file, which is far cheaper.

## Where it falls down

**The weight.** Six prompts measured by hand run from 6,574 characters (Terminal) to 32,588
(Maximalism) — up to roughly eight thousand tokens in a single paste, at four characters to the token. Not
catastrophic, but it is a medium-sized file of your project competing for the model's attention with the
code you actually want it to look at. That is why step two above is not optional.

**The stack.** All of it is written in React and Tailwind vocabulary: classes like `px-8 py-4`, scales like
`text-8xl`, `var(--foreground)` variables, references to shadcn/ui and to Lucide's `strokeWidth`. If you
work in Flutter, SwiftUI, or Vue with your own CSS, ask it first to convert the tokens section to your
format, and only then to apply the style. Let it improvise and you end up with Tailwind class names written
into a file that has no Tailwind.

**The gallery's Light/Dark filter lies in two places.** Academia and Maximalism are labelled as light
styles and both demos are unmistakably dark — Academia is deep brown with gold, Maximalism is black with
neon — and the prompt modal itself declares them Dark Mode. They are two of the most interesting entries in
the catalogue, and filtering by Dark hides them from you.

## Before it goes into client work

There is no terms page and no licence anywhere on the site. The material is plainly put there to be
copied, but "free and frictionless" is not the same as "licensed for commercial use". If this is going into
a repository you bill someone for, ask the author in writing rather than assume.

## Who it serves, and who it half-serves

It serves you enormously if you build landing pages, marketing sites, portfolios or the public face of a
product, in React with Tailwind. That is where it drops in without friction and where the difference
between using it and not using it shows up in the first render.

It half-serves you if you are building an application. All thirty demos are marketing pages: not one app
screen, no dashboard, no dense table, no long form, no empty state. The tokens and components still help,
but each style's non-negotiable decisions are calibrated for a page you read top to bottom once, and
one-hundred-and-sixty-pixel type is a different proposition when your user is going to sit inside it for
six hours.

## Verdict

Worth the session it takes to try. Even if you use none of the thirty as they come, reading two or three of
these documents teaches you the level of detail an agent needs before design stops coming out generic — and
that lesson you take, for free, to every project after this one.
